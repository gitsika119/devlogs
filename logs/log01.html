I'm pursuing Computer Engineering with my eyes on software and cybersecurity roles. Which begs the question: why not just take Computer Science? Why go through the nightmare of soldering and crashing out over physical components?

Because it's fun T-T

As nerdy as it may sound, my few hands-on projects have been a really (if not the most) enjoyable part of my degree, and while I don't see myself building and soldering full-time (I don't have the hand eye coordination to do that 9-5), I do enjoy it on the occasions I do it.

Like all engineers, I started small:

  • Lighting a bulb on a breadboard
  • Building with logic gates and resistors
  • Adding capacitors
  • ...and BOOM trying to solder everything together on a PCB while my TA watches, deeply concerned about the safety of the components. (Not my finger, which accidentally touched the soldering gun tip. "Rite of passage," apparently.)

EE 210: The Archaic Music Player

For my EE 210 final project, I built a "music player."

It was completely archaic. Nobody would use it to actually listen to music today, but seeing how everyday devices start out as a sprawling mess on a bench is incredible. It's a snapshot of the foundational layers before everything gets shrunk down.

Trying to transport a fully wired analog prototype is an engineering challenge in itself. One loose jumper wire inside a backpack and the entire circuit is dead on arrival at the lab bench.

Moving from that fragile mess of wires to a permanent printed circuit board changes everything. There is a steep learning curve to getting clean solder joints and making sure your ICs are seated correctly, but the structural reliability is unmatched.

STM32: Brick Breaker

My second hardware project shifted away from discrete analog chaos toward integrated embedded systems. The IEEE chapter at Penn State hosted a workshop series on the STM32 microcontroller, concluding with building a functional game of Brick Breaker.

Soldering the components together, flashing the code, and watching it come to life puts the scale of the field into perspective. You look at this custom PCB plugged into a laptop playing a retro game, and then you realize this exact same STM32 chip powers hoverboards.

Knowing how much there is left to learn, and seeing how fast this technology has progressed just makes me happy to say that I'll be an engineer in the future.

Final Thoughts

Software gets a lot of credit for abstraction, but there is a distinct satisfaction in abstracting away a mess of wires into a single pocket-sized gizmo.
