As someone who started off as a CS major, I had a very software-centered view of CyberSec. I forgot that 'cyber' includes not only the software and networks related to the computer, but the computer itself. The hardware.

Starting with a line I wrote in log 02: "When features on a chip are only a few nanometers wide, hardware-level vulnerabilities (like supply chain injections, hardware trojans, or physical side-channel attacks) become terrifying, near-invisible threats."

Now, you'd think that the chips being tiny was a pro; who'll go and make the effort to tamper with something so complicated? Something that requires a multi-million dollar machine to build must definitely need the same resources to tamper with, right?

Wrong.

Most companies, as we saw previously, don't build chips themselves; the actual physical fabrication is outsourced. This means that the long, convoluted supply chain of the chip manufacturing process is a massive vulnerability. Just ONE subtle alteration to the digital layout mask (photomask) before printing can create a hardware trojan that compromises the security of the entire chip. The cost to the attacker is essentially zero—they are just riding on the multi-billion dollar infrastructure you paid for.

This is especially concerning for government and military applications, where a single compromised batch of chips could lead to... well, problems. And the worst part? These trojans can be designed to stay dormant until a specific trigger wakes them up, making them nearly impossible to detect through standard post-fabrication testing.

The existential dread of hardware security is that because the backdoor is baked directly into the logic gates, your software layer is entirely blind to it. Your kernel patches, anti-virus sweeps, and secure boot profiles are completely useless if the silicon beneath them is lying to them.

It’s a unique kind of cooked. You can’t push an update to physically un-modify a cluster of microscopic transistors inside a sealed package. Once it’s printed, it’s permanent.

Don't Worry, Someone is Stressing (So We Don't Have To)

To be fair, it's not a complete free-for-all. There are rooms full of incredibly stressed engineers and defense labs trying their absolute best to stop this. They run massive mathematical proofs before fabrication, pay absurd premiums for top-secret domestic foundries, and literally destroy random sample chips from batches by slicing them open with electron microscopes just to check the gate alignments pixel-by-pixel against the original blueprints.

People are trying. Very hard.

But here is the punchline for the rest of us: unless you are running a military-grade aerospace defense lab, you can't do anything about it. Your phone, your laptop, your smart fridge, nobody is shaving those down with ion beams to look for an extra 50 gates hidden among 50 billion. We just live with the statistical probability that the global supply chain was honest enough this morning.

It forces you, a software user/operator/creator, into a completely dismal direction: How do I write code that survives on a system I don't trust?

Spoiler alert: you don't. If the hardware is rigged, it's already over. Just hit compile and don't think too hard about it. :D
